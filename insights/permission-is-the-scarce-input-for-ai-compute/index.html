Permission is the scarce input for AI compute

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 13 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Permission to operate is the consent a host community grants a data center to use its land, power and water on stated terms. It is not a public relations exercise. In the week of 3 November 2026 it became a measurable planning input, as binding as a grid connection, because voters and protesters showed they can stop funded capacity.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the Ballotpedia count of local measures decided on 3 November 2026, the OpenAI National Community Compact published on 5 November 2026, reporting of the Amsterdam protest on 6 November 2026, and the International Energy Agency outlook. Every figure traces to one of those sources.

The vote changed the constraint

Ballotpedia counted 41 local ballot measures on data centers on 3 November. Voters favored limiting development in 40 of them. The sole exception was Harwood in North Dakota, where voters defeated a proposed review requirement for certain developments including data centers.

Voters approved 27 measures that limited development or expressed opposition to it, including five full bans, twelve partial bans and three moratoriums lasting six months in Maine. They also defeated 13 measures that would have favored development. This was a pattern across ten states, not one town objecting to one design.

Europe shows the same pressure in a different form

Three days later the same pressure appeared in Amsterdam. Police said 218 people were arrested on suspicion of trespassing after protesters entered a construction site in Amsterdam West on 6 November, and all were released by the next day. The site is being developed by Pure DC and leased to Microsoft. Pure DC says the project, called AMS01, comprises three towers of 26 megawatts each, or 78 megawatts in total, and investment of more than one billion euros.

The protest was part of a wider European campaign connected to Stop Dirty Data Centres, a coalition calling for a moratorium on large data centers. Where American opponents used the ballot, European opponents used direct action. The underlying questions were the same. Who pays for the power. Who bears the pressure on the local grid. What does the host place gain.

Why the numbers make consent local

The International Energy Agency estimates that data centers used about 415 terawatt hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5 percent of global use, and projects about 945 terawatt hours by 2030 in its base case. Globally that remains under 3 percent of expected demand. Locally it arrives as one very large load in one place.

That concentration explains the politics. A resident does not experience a global share. A resident experiences a substation, a water plan, construction traffic and a household bill. When concentrated costs land locally while benefits flow mostly elsewhere, consent decides whether the project timetable is real.

What OpenAI conceded and what it did not

OpenAI published its compact on 5 November, two days after the vote. For future campuses it owns and operates, it pledged to pay the full cost of the electricity it uses and the infrastructure needed to serve its sites, with financial assurances before major spending begins so other customers are protected if a project is delayed, reduced or cancelled. It said it would publish site level electricity and water use, commission independent noise studies, report local employment, and stop requiring public officials to sign nondisclosure agreements.

Its separate blueprint asks lawmakers to apply comparable rules across the industry, including large load tariffs set through state utility commissions. Read the limits as carefully as the promises. The compact covers campuses OpenAI owns and operates, while much capacity built for its services is owned by partners, and reporting noted no enforcement mechanism beyond annual public reporting. A voluntary compact is a start, not yet a guarantee a community can enforce.

Price permission into the plan

Treat permission as a workstream with an owner, a budget and a date, not as communications support after the engineering plan is fixed. Sequence it before land and power commitments become irreversible.

Publish the local cost account early. State who pays for grid work, what protection exists if the project shrinks or stops, how much water the design will use, and what local jobs are expected. Put those figures in public documents and update them on a fixed schedule.

Design for the objections voters raised. Prefer cooling that uses little water, commit to flexible demand during grid stress, and make noise and traffic studies independent. OpenAI cited up to 1,000 megawatts of flexible demand response at its campus in Effingham in Georgia, and 80 million dollars for community projects in Effingham County and 40 million dollars in Pike County in Ohio. Local benefit must be specific, funded and visible.

For buyers of compute, ask each provider which announced capacity has planning consent, grid terms and community commitments in place. Discount capacity that lacks all three.

Jengara decision rule

Approve an AI infrastructure plan only when permission is evidenced alongside power and capital: documented community terms, full local infrastructure costs assigned to the project, public reporting on power and water use, and a credible pause or exit that protects residents if the project changes. If a plan can show funding and accelerators but cannot show consent, treat its capacity as unbuilt and its date as unproven.

Executive takeaway

The scarce input has shifted. Chips can be ordered and capital raised. Consent must be earned place by place, and it now decides which announced gigawatts become real.
