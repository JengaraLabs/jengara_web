Bring your own power is the new grid

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 9 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Bring your own power means a data center operator generates electricity on its own site instead of waiting for a utility to connect it to the grid. The generator in its newest form is a jet engine from a retired aircraft, rebuilt to burn natural gas. The operator is no longer buying a connection. It is buying time, and accepting the duties of a power producer.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed TechCrunch reporting on 3 November 2026 of a New York Times investigation, IEEE Spectrum reporting on the ProEnergy PE6000, and GlobalData analysis drawing on the International Energy Agency Electricity 2026 outlook. Every figure in this note traces to one of those sources.

The queue turned time into the scarce asset

The International Energy Agency counts more than 2,500 gigawatts of renewable generation, large load and storage projects waiting in grid connection queues worldwide, in figures reported by GlobalData from the Electricity 2026 outlook. GlobalData adds that building new grid infrastructure can take five to 15 years, against one to three years to build a data center, and that developers in the main European hubs can wait seven to 10 years for a connection.

The wait is now costing deals. Connecting 100 megawatts in Northern Virginia takes about seven years, on the International Energy Agency figure cited by the Times. Last month Microsoft pulled out as anchor customer for the Nscale site in Loughton, England, after the utility said it could not deliver enough power until the early to mid 2030s. An executive at Mitsubishi Power told Bloomberg most new power capacity is expected only by 2031.

A bridge product with a bridge clock

ProEnergy sells the clearest version. Its PE6000 rebuilds the core of a GE engine first built for Boeing jets to burn natural gas and delivers 48 megawatts per unit, according to IEEE Spectrum. Spectrum reports 21 turbines sold for two data center projects amounting to more than one gigawatt, with both projects expecting five to seven years of bridging power until grid interconnection arrives.

The Times found eight converted turbines installed in Mississippi to help power the xAI Colossus 2 site in Memphis, citing state permits and satellite imagery. OpenAI used gas turbines at its first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas, in 2025, and its builder Crusoe ordered 13 ProEnergy units in April. ProEnergy has fabricated 75 PE6000 packages since 2020, with 52 more in assembly or on order.

Speed imports four exposures

The first is efficiency. Converted engines are less efficient and pollute more than turbines designed for conventional gas plants, and more of them are needed to make the same power, according to the Times reporting summarised by TechCrunch. Fuel a utility once procured at scale must now be secured and priced by the operator.

The second is permission. GlobalData warns that grid congestion is pushing operators toward on site gas power, which risks increasing emissions even as grids get cleaner. TechCrunch notes experts expect efficiency and environmental concerns to grow in importance within a year or two, so a permit won quickly may not stay uncontested.

The third is supply. Waiting lists for large new turbines run three to five years at GE Vernova and Siemens Energy, on the account Paul Browning, who once led GE Power, gave to Spectrum. Against that, ProEnergy says a PE6000 can start in five minutes and be swapped within 72 hours for maintenance. The service promise is much of the product.

The fourth is the exit. Spectrum reports that when grid power arrives these machines can become backup, supplement the grid, or be sold to the local utility. None of those endings is automatic. A bridge with no priced ending is a permanent plant bought by accident.

Price the bridge as a bridge

Keep bridge power in its own line of the investment case. State what the units and their fuel cost, what starting years earlier earns, and what the grid delay being replaced would cost. Run the case at the grid date committed in writing, and again with that date moved two years in each direction.

Contract for the ending at the start. Fix who owns the units when grid power arrives, what credit follows if they move to backup duty or are sold, who pays for removal, and what happens to gas contracts that outlive the bridge.

Ask five questions before you approve it

First, what firm grid date sits behind the bridge period, and which document proves it. Second, what output the units will deliver on the hottest local day, tested rather than nameplate. Third, which emissions limits apply in writing, and who pays if they tighten. Fourth, how much gas is contracted, at what price risk, for how long. Fifth, what ending is contracted for every unit on the day grid power arrives.

Jengara decision rule

Approve bring your own power only as a priced bridge, with a firm grid date behind it, a contracted ending for every unit, and fuel and emissions exposure stated beside the revenue from starting early. If a proposal treats converted engines as a permanent answer, or cannot price each month of delay the bridge replaces, send it back. Speed is worth buying. An unpriced power business is not.

Executive takeaway

The grid queue did not just delay the AI buildout. It moved power generation inside the data center investment case, where most operators have never run it.
