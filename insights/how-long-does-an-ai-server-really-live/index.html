How long does an AI server really live

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 3 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Useful life is the number of years a company expects a server to stay productive, and it decides how fast the cost of that server reaches the income statement. Extend the life and annual depreciation falls and reported profit rises. Shorten it and the opposite happens. In January 2025 two large buyers of AI hardware moved that assumption in opposite directions.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the filings record compiled by WireSift Research, Amazon disclosure in its quarterly filing, the Harvard Business School case on Meta published in June 2026, and the Reuters Breakingviews sensitivity published on 29 October 2026. Every figure traces to those sources.

Two buyers moved in opposite directions

Effective 1 January 2025, Amazon shortened the estimated useful life of a subset of its servers and networking equipment from six years to five years. Amazon said the shorter lives were due to the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning. The change increased depreciation in 2025 by about 1.4 billion dollars and reduced net income by about 1.0 billion dollars.

Meta moved the other way on the same date. It extended the estimated useful life of most of its servers and network assets to 5.5 years, up from a prior range of four to five years. Meta expected that one change to reduce depreciation in 2025 by 2.92 billion dollars and to lift net income by 2.59 billion dollars, or 1.00 dollar per share.

The extension came first

Amazon was the exception, not the trend. Since 2022, Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle all stretched assumed server lives toward six years. WireSift, reading the annual filings, found that in the year each company made its change the lift to net income ran between 4.0 and 4.6 percent. The disclosed gains were 3.0 billion dollars at Microsoft, 3.0 billion dollars at Alphabet, 2.5 billion dollars at Amazon, 2.59 billion dollars at Meta and 0.57 billion dollars at Oracle.

Every change was disclosed, and each can be defended if the hardware stays productive for longer. The harder question is whether the longer life is an observation about the fleet, or a forecast that demand for older capacity will hold up.

The bill has not yet arrived in full

Scale is why a small change in years now moves large numbers. Across the same five companies, WireSift put capital spending in the trailing twelve months at about 482 billion dollars, nearly four times depreciation of about 125 billion dollars. Companies are adding AI hardware far faster than they are expensing it, so current earnings reflect an older and cheaper fleet than the one now being installed. The gap closes at the pace the useful life assumption sets.

Reuters Breakingviews tested what happens if that pace proves too slow. Its estimate was that if Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft each shortened assumed lives by just one year, combined net income could fall by about 36 billion dollars in 2027. That sensitivity shows how much reported profit rests on a judgment no outsider can directly observe.

Running is not the same as earning

A chip can still power on and still have lost much of its economic value, because a newer chip does the same work with less power and at a lower cost per token. Management teams answer that older chips move down the stack to inference and to less demanding work, where they keep earning. Critics answer that Nvidia now works to an annual architecture rhythm, with Blackwell followed by Rubin in 2026 and Rubin Ultra planned for 2027, so the efficiency gap widens every year.

Investor Michael Burry has argued that the extensions understate depreciation by about 176 billion dollars between 2026 and 2028. WireSift notes that this is his own forward projection and that it does not match the filings, where disclosed single year impacts run at about 4 to 5 percent of profit. The debate cannot be settled from the accounts alone. None of the five companies reports graphics processors as a separate asset class. The chips sit inside a blended category with servers and networking equipment, and utilisation by chip vintage is not disclosed.

What to test before you trust the margin

Start with cash, because depreciation involves no cash outflow in the period and none of these changes alters free cash flow. Then ask for the evidence behind the life. Which share of each chip vintage is still in service, and what does each vintage cost in power for the work it serves? Run the one year sensitivity on any AI infrastructure margin you are shown. A margin built on six years and a margin built on five are not the same margin, even when the hardware underneath is identical.

Buyers should apply the same test to contracts. Ask how a provider depreciates the fleet behind your price. If you build your own, set the life from measured utilisation for each vintage, and review it when a new generation ships.

Jengara decision rule

Treat useful life as an operating claim, not an accounting footnote. Accept a five or six year life only where utilisation, revenue and power cost by hardware vintage prove the fleet still earns in its later years. Price every AI infrastructure decision on the margin that survives a one year shortening, and keep the longer life as upside. If evidence by vintage cannot be produced, the shorter life is the honest one.

Executive takeaway

Reported AI profit now depends on how long hardware is assumed to earn. The difference is evidence by vintage, and that is what investors and buyers should demand.
