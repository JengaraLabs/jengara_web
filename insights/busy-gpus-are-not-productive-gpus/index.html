Busy GPUs are not productive GPUs

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 19 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Busy is not the same as productive. GPU utilisation records that a chip had work to do. It does not record whether that work was new, finished on time, or had to be done again because the context it needed had been thrown away.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the 451 Research Pathfinder paper From inference to agentic AI from November 2026, the Inference Economics Scorecard from WEKA and NAND Research launched on 17 November 2026, the Cast AI 2026 State of Kubernetes Optimization Report, and McKinsey work on AI workloads. Every figure traces to one of those sources.

Inference has taken the spending lead

451 Research projects that inference will drive 225 billion dollars, or 34 percent, of AI infrastructure spending in 2026, rising to 746 billion dollars, or 46 percent, by 2030.

McKinsey reaches the same direction from power. It expects inference demand to grow at 35 percent a year and pass 90 gigawatts by 2030, while training grows at 22 percent a year and passes 60 gigawatts.

Agents sharpen the shift. In North America alone, 451 Research forecasts tokens produced rising from 24 quadrillion in 2026 to 206 quadrillion in 2028, with the agent share climbing from 32 percent to 72 percent. A workload that runs all day, in many steps, for many users cannot be judged with a metric built for training.

Busy can mean repeating old work

An agent does not simply answer once. It works in steps, and at each step it reads again the context built in earlier steps. When that context has been evicted from GPU memory for other sessions, the GPU must rebuild it before the task can move forward. The chip looks fully used. The task has made no progress.

The Nebius benchmark published with the scorecard shows the hidden tax. On one node with eight NVIDIA HGX B300 GPUs, serving a DeepSeek model to 512 active sessions from more than 5,000 real agentic coding sessions, GPU memory alone recomputed 60 percent of prefill work from context already processed. With a shared context tier, that share fell to 6.8 percent.

The shared tier then produced 3.7 times the output tokens per GPU hour on the same GPUs, and 97 percent of successfully completed requests reached a first token within five seconds, up from 44 percent. Same silicon. Radically different finished work.

Idle can hide inside the same fleet

The opposite failure proves the same point. Cast AI reports average GPU utilisation of just 5 percent across tens of thousands of production Kubernetes clusters on AWS, Azure and Google Cloud in its 2026 report, with CPU at 8 percent and memory at 20 percent.

One fleet sits almost idle because whole GPUs are allocated to workloads that use a fraction of them. Another reports high utilisation while spending most cycles rebuilding context. One number cannot tell a board which problem it owns.

Adding GPUs is a weak first response. 451 Research notes that buyers face hardware scarcity, memory limits, and limits on power, cooling and floor space at once. Capacity bought against the wrong diagnosis leaves the bottleneck untouched.

Measure finished work, not activity

The Inference Economics Scorecard from WEKA and NAND Research offers a better report card. Its nine measures include tokens per watt, tokens per rack, cost per inference, GPU goodput, time to first token, memory efficiency, operational density, agent concurrency and context retention. Goodput counts only output that meets the promised quality and latency terms.

Its testing discipline matters as much. Systems must face the same workload and targets. The whole serving stack must be measured, not the chip alone. Runs must last at least eight hours, and only successful output counts.

Apply that discipline internally first. Require every inference service to report completed requests at its latency target, cost per completed request, tokens per watt, and the share of work recomputed, over a full working day.

Buy and size for goodput

Buyers are already changing what they purchase. Among organisations that run inference in production, 41 percent buy fundamentally different hardware and software than for the rest of their IT estate, according to 451 Research. Nearly four in ten, 39 percent, now buy a dedicated AI data infrastructure platform as a distinct component.

Procurement should change its evidence standard to match. Ask each vendor to run your own traces for at least eight hours at your concurrency level. Price the result per completed request at your latency target, per watt and per rack, and publish the failure count beside throughput.

Size the estate for finished work over a working day under sustained load, and hold memory and context design to the same review as accelerator count. Context loss becomes repeated compute on hardware already paid for.

Jengara decision rule

Do not approve inference capacity on utilisation or peak throughput. Approve it on goodput proven over at least eight hours on work that resembles your own: completed requests at the promised latency, counted with every failure, priced per watt, per rack and per dollar. Without that number, GPUs may be busy, with no evidence they are productive.

Executive takeaway

Utilisation was a training era metric. Inference pays for finished work, and agents can keep expensive silicon busy while producing little of it. Boards should ask for goodput first and treat capacity requests without it as unpriced.
