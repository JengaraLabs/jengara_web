Stopping an agent is not recovery

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 15 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Recovery is the ability to return data, applications, identities and workflows to a trusted state after an agent has acted. A stop ends future action. Recovery repairs past action, and new evidence shows that repair is where cost now concentrates.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the Kore.ai Agent Productivity Index 2026, based on a survey of 408 IT and business leaders, the Menlo Ventures 2026 State of Enterprise AI report, the CrowdStrike AI Kill Switch announcement, and the Commvault and Zscaler announcements, all published in November 2026. Every figure traces to one of those sources.

Agents now act where reversal hurts

Kore.ai found that 82 percent of enterprises report their agents have autonomously executed consequential actions in production, including financial transactions, data migrations and system updates. Agents operate where a mistake moves money, changes records or alters access.

Scale explains the urgency. Menlo Ventures estimates deployment spending reached an annualised run rate of 176.8 billion dollars by September 2026, up 5.4 times in a year, and reports that agents now generate 80 percent of tokens on OpenRouter.

The reversal bill is the hidden operating cost

Kore.ai reports that 79.4 percent of consequential autonomous actions needed manual reversal, and 93.2 percent of those reversals were moderately or very costly. A manual reversal means people must identify what happened, trace affected systems, undo the action and validate effects downstream.

Price the expected reversal in the business case.

Safeguards existed and reversals still happened

This is not a story of enterprises with no controls. Kore.ai found that 91.4 percent of organisations had safeguards in place before the action that later needed reversal. In the same survey, 87 percent report a formal kill switch or containment policy, and 88 percent say they can pause a live agent without breaking dependent systems. Yet 72 percent believe agents introduce unmanaged financial or compliance risk.

CrowdStrike announced its AI Kill Switch in Falcon Guardian on 3 November 2026. An authorised administrator can terminate a running agent, block it from relaunching and cut off its network access, with every action recorded in an audit trail. Termination runs first at the Gateway, and soon on the endpoint. A gateway stop does not undo a transaction already completed or restore data already changed.

Detection is faster than attribution

Recovery begins with knowing which agent acted and what it touched. Kore.ai reports that 50 percent of organisations detect malfunctions within one to four hours, and 33 percent take four to eight hours. But 70 percent could not identify the responsible agent when a failure occurred where many agents were involved, and 15 percent rely primarily on end users to report problems.

The consequences are commercial. Agent failures led to revenue loss for 41.7 percent of respondents, service level agreement violations for 30.9 percent and customer churn for 27.5 percent. Without that trace, a team cannot scope recovery. It can only search.

Recovery is becoming a product category

Vendors have started selling the missing half. On 10 November 2026 Commvault and Zscaler announced a partnership pairing Zscaler discovery and policy enforcement with Commvault recovery for data, applications and identities. Read the status carefully. The companies present this as planned collaboration, not as a generally available joint product.

Commvault gave more detail in its separate AgentRecover announcement. It is designed to discover agents, map what they interact with, audit what an agent did and recover both the agent and the affected resources, concentrating on what the agent changed. Early access is targeted for Microsoft Foundry in November 2026 and for Amazon Bedrock AgentCore in December 2026.

Build recovery before granting authority

Map the reverse path before deployment. For every consequential action, write down how it is undone, who approves restoration and how long it should take. If no reverse path exists, reduce the authority until one does.

Make attribution a condition of action, then test it. Every consequential action should carry a trace naming the agent, the tool, the identity used, the approval relied on and the resources changed. Pick a completed action at random and ask the team to reconstruct it in full. Rehearse the stop, the trace and the restoration together, and time each step.

Classify the failure correctly. Only 14 percent of organisations have a dedicated AI operations or governance team, while 54 percent classify failures as IT incidents and only 25 percent as operational risk. When an agent touches revenue or customers, route the response to the owner of that risk.

Jengara decision rule

Approve an agent for consequential work only when the team can show, in a live test, how it will stop the agent, name the agent that acted, list everything it changed and restore each changed resource to a trusted state within a stated time. If the plan ends at the kill switch, treat the agent as unready for consequential authority.

Executive takeaway

The question has moved on from whether an agent can be stopped. Boards should now ask what it will cost to undo what the agent already did, and who has rehearsed that undoing.
