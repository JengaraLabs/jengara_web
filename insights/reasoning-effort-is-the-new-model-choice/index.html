Reasoning effort is the new model choice

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 7 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Reasoning effort is the setting that tells a model how much internal thinking to do before it answers. The model writes reasoning tokens that the user never sees, the caller pays for them at output rates, and only then does the visible answer arrive. One setting therefore changes three things at once. How good the answer is. How long the user waits. How much each task costs.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index version 5 measurements as reported on 28 October 2026, the vendor documentation summarised in that reporting, and a 2026 study of reasoning effort in agents by researchers at Johns Hopkins University, Together AI and Cornell University. Every figure traces to those sources.

The same model is now several products

Artificial Analysis benchmarks each effort level as a separate entry, and as of 28 October 2026 the five leading models cost 4.4 to 13.5 times as much per task at their highest setting as at their lowest. Claude Opus 5.5 scores 35 at low effort for 0.98 dollars per task and 58 at max for 10.04 dollars.

That spread rivals the gap between models. GPT 6.1 Sol at max scores 55, the same score as the more expensive GPT 6 Astra at high, for 1.27 dollars per task against 3.24 dollars. Choosing a model without choosing its effort level is now an incomplete purchase.

The gain flattens while the bill accelerates

In all five models, the step from low to medium gave the largest score gain of any step, from 6 points on Claude Haiku 5.5 to 13 points on Claude Opus 5.5. The early thinking pays.

The step from xhigh to max was the most expensive step in all five, taking 43 percent to 63 percent of each model cost increase from low to max, and it added 1 to 3 points, or 6 points on one model. At medium, all five start answering within about 15 seconds. At max, none starts within two minutes, and Claude Opus 5.5 takes about 11 minutes before its first visible output.

Higher effort can lose what lower effort solved

The Together AI study varied effort while holding tasks, prompts and the agent harness fixed across four benchmarks and eight models. On one agent benchmark, every model failed at its highest effort on some tasks it had solved at its lowest. In one case, moving from high to xhigh lost eight tasks while newly solving only five, so the solved count fell from 76 to 73 of 89 tasks.

On tasks solved at every level, mean cost at higher effort reached up to 42 times the cost at the lowest level, driven by more computation per call and by longer agent trajectories. Turning reasoning off did not reliably save money either, because agents compensated with longer trajectories.

You pay for thinking you cannot see

OpenAI states that reasoning tokens are not visible through its API, but they still occupy space in the context window and are billed as output tokens. Anthropic bills the full thinking of Claude even when the response carries only a summary, and Google bills the thinking of Gemini the same way. The buyer funds work the buyer cannot inspect, which makes measurement the only control.

Defaults decide for teams that never choose. Medium is the documented default for four of the five models in the table, and Claude Opus 5.5 defaults to medium, one level below the default of Claude Opus 5. Level names do not travel between models either. Anthropic recalibrated the levels of Claude Sonnet 5.5, so the same name now buys a different amount of thinking than it did on Claude Sonnet 5.

Set effort per feature, not per company

Start with one feature and build an evaluation set from its real traffic, with checks in code. Run that set at the default level and at one level on either side, and repeat each run. For every task, record whether it passed, its tokens, its cost, and its wait.

Divide cost by pass rate to get cost per passing task, and choose the cheapest level that clears the quality bar of the feature within its latency budget. Store the level in configuration next to the model name, and rerun the sweep when the model or prompt changes.

Ladder upward instead of locking at the top

The Together AI study tested a simple alternative it calls naive laddering. Start at the lowest effort, check the result, and escalate only after a failed check. Laddering matched or improved the success of fixed top effort across all models and benchmarks in the study, at a lower median inference cost once waiting time was counted. Models cannot reliably choose their own effort from a task description alone, so the system should check outcomes instead.

Where no reliable check exists, treat the default level as the ceiling for routine work and reserve higher effort for tasks where the cost of failure is known and high.

Jengara decision rule

Set reasoning effort per feature, in configuration, from your own evaluation. Buy the cheapest level that passes that evaluation within the latency budget, ladder upward only after a failed check, and treat any proposal to run at max by default as a spending proposal that must show its gain in score, cost per passing task and wait. If that evidence cannot be produced, medium is the ceiling and max is the exception.

Executive takeaway

Model choice used to be the purchase. Now the purchase is a model at an effort level, and the gap between levels can exceed the gap between models.
