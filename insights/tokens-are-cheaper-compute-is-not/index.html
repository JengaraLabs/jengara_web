Tokens are cheaper. Compute is not.

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 26 October 2026 · Product and engineering notes

The inference price paradox is the split between what a token costs and what the capacity behind it costs. In October 2026 the two prices moved in opposite directions at speed. Hosted models became dramatically cheaper to call, while the graphics processors that run models became dramatically more expensive to rent. Any plan that treats AI compute as one market getting cheaper is now reading only half the price sheet.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the a16z Charts of the Week rental series built on Ornn data as of 19 October 2026, Silicon Data rental indexes reported for 10 October 2026, the Nebius published price list effective 1 October 2026, provider rate cards collected in mid October 2026, and Anthropic pricing for Claude Haiku 5.5 released on 7 October 2026. Every figure in this note traces to one of those sources, listed in full below.

Two prices, two directions

Start with the token. Anthropic released Claude Haiku 5.5 on 7 October 2026 at 0.10 dollars per million input tokens and 0.50 dollars per million output tokens for prompts up to 100,000 tokens. That is a tenth of the price of Claude Haiku 4.5, which listed at 1 dollar and 5 dollars, and Anthropic puts the average saving at around 75 percent once real workloads are counted. Now look at the capacity underneath. A rental series published by a16z and built on Ornn data, indexed to 1 January 2026, ends in mid October with the Nvidia H200 line near 260, the B200 near 220, and the H100 near 135. In plain terms, H200 rental costs rose about 160 percent this year, B200 costs rose about 120 percent, and H100 costs rose about 35 percent. Even the six year old A100 rents for more than in January. Tokens are a software price set by competition. Capacity is a physical price set by scarcity, and scarcity is winning.

The rental market in numbers

Silicon Data neocloud indexes stood on 10 October 2026 at 2.82 dollars per GPU hour for the H100, 3.43 dollars for the H200, 5.93 dollars for the B200 and 6.71 dollars for the B300. Medians from provider pricing pages on 16 October 2026 put on demand rates at 4.25 dollars for the H100, 5.70 dollars for the H200, 8.35 dollars for the B200 and 9.75 dollars for the B300. The spread for the same B300 chip in October comparisons ran from 7.10 dollars per GPU hour at Modal to about 17.80 dollars on AWS.

One price list shows the mechanism

Nebius makes the mechanism visible because it published the change. On 1 October 2026 its on demand rate for the H100 rose from 3.85 dollars to 4.50 dollars per GPU hour, the H200 rose from 4.50 dollars to 5.40 dollars, the B200 rose about 19 percent to 8.50 dollars, and the B300 rose from 7.85 dollars to 9.50 dollars. The increases run from about 17 percent to about 21 percent in a single step, and they apply to old and new chips alike. A provider building capacity at full speed would normally cut prices to fill it. Raising prices on every generation at once signals the opposite. Demand is absorbing supply faster than supply arrives, and buyers should stop budgeting for relief the market has not promised.

Why scarce capacity keeps its price

The H200 leads the rises for a physical reason. It carries 141 gigabytes of high bandwidth memory and 4.8 terabytes per second of memory bandwidth, about 1.76 times the memory capacity of the H100 it follows. Large models that fit on one H200 must be split across more H100s, so the scarcer chip saves system cost as well as time. CBRE figures for the first half of 2026 put primary market data center vacancy at 1.4 percent, with 80.4 percent of capacity under construction already preleased. Silicon Data figures reported in September 2026 put the resale value of the B200 at 158 percent of its launch price, about a year after broad availability began. Reporting in late October described new AI labs that locked in capacity early realising they could resell it at a profit if their model work failed. Capacity has become an asset that appreciates, and assets that appreciate do not get cheaper to rent.

What leaders should do now

Four moves follow. First, split the budget into two lines. Plan model API spend on falling token prices, and plan rented capacity on current rental indexes and rate cards, refreshed each quarter. Second, lock in steady workloads early and keep burst demand on hosted APIs, where falling token prices do the work for you. Late commitment in this market is expensive even when it is right. Third, attack utilisation before buying more hours. At 9.50 dollars per B300 hour on a published list, idle rented capacity is now a material line item, and scheduling, batching and shutting down idle instances pay back faster than in January. Fourth, shop the spread, because provider choice and contract shape can matter more than chip choice.

Jengara decision rule

Plan tokens and capacity as two markets. Assume token prices keep falling and capacity prices stay firm until rental indexes prove otherwise. If a business case needs rented GPUs to get cheaper next year in order to work, the business case does not work yet.

Executive takeaway

The cheapest token in AI history now runs on expensive rented capacity. Budget for both facts at once.
