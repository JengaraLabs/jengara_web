The AI buildout is now a credit story

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 28 October 2026 · Product and engineering notes

A credit story is about who pays when timing goes wrong. The AI buildout has become one. Spending once funded from operating cash now relies on bonds, project vehicles, leases and guarantees, so the question has shifted from whether capacity can be built to who carries the loss if revenue arrives late.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed a Moody’s Ratings assessment reported by Data Centre Dynamics, Reuters reporting on Meta Hyperion in Louisiana, a CNBC analysis of Nvidia filings from late October, an IIGCC note on data centre financing published on 27 October 2026, and a synthesis of filings and market data by Luminix. Every figure traces to one of those sources, listed below.

Cash stopped being enough

Moody’s expects capital spending by six large players to reach about 700 billion dollars in 2026 and about 870 billion dollars in 2027, roughly six times the level in 2022. IIGCC puts spending by the five largest hyperscalers at a projected 800 billion dollars in 2026, up from 97 billion dollars in 2020. Cash generation has not kept pace with commitments of that size. IIGCC notes that hyperscaler bond issuance reached about 170 billion dollars by late June 2026, against an annual average of 35 billion dollars between 2020 and 2024. Luminix, citing J.P. Morgan Asset Management, puts gross bond issuance at about 17 billion dollars in 2024, 109 billion dollars in 2025 and 194 billion dollars in the first half of 2026 alone.

The debt moved beyond the headline balance sheet

Much new borrowing sits in separate vehicles that own a campus and borrow against a long lease. Reuters described the template at Meta Hyperion in Louisiana, where bonds were issued through Beignet Investor and Meta holds a 20 percent minority stake, so project liabilities stay off its balance sheet. Luminix reports that the vehicle sold 27.3 billion dollars of notes that Meta does not consolidate, while Meta discloses maximum exposure to loss of about 45.95 billion dollars through its lease, its equity and a residual value guarantee. Reuters also cited a Morgan Stanley estimate of about 3 trillion dollars in similar structures across large AI companies. Off balance sheet does not mean off risk. It means the obligation must be found before it can be judged.

Guarantees turned suppliers into financiers

The supplier layer now carries credit as well as product risk. Reporting of a CNBC analysis of Nvidia filings described 36 billion dollars in off balance sheet AI cloud agreements, a guarantee of up to 105 billion dollars for an OpenAI data centre in Ohio, and 20 billion dollars of data centre leases signed as tenant for later assignment. The same reporting put Nvidia equity stakes at about 99 billion dollars in the second quarter and noted that CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN shares had each fallen at least 30 percent in October. Phys.org cited a central bank analysis finding that 55.2 percent of investments in AI firms came from other AI firms. When a supplier invests in a customer, guarantees its site and sells it chips, demand and credit become the same exposure.

Pricing now follows the tenant, not the building

Lenders now price that reality. Luminix shows CoreWeave borrowing against the same class of Nvidia hardware at very different costs depending on the customer behind the contract, from 2.25 percentage points over SOFR for investment grade customers to 5.50 percentage points for a group rated below investment grade. The building matters mainly if the tenant stops paying, so tenant credit sets the price. Timing adds pressure. Moody’s notes that a data centre typically takes 12 to 24 months from spending to revenue. Luminix reports that S and P cut Oracle to one notch above junk on 9 July 2026 and that OpenAI accounts for roughly half of Oracle contracted revenue backlog. A late campus does not pause interest. It only pauses income.

What leaders should do now

Four moves follow. First, map full exposure in one view, including bonds, leases not yet started, guarantees, residual value promises and purchase commitments, rather than reading debt from the headline balance sheet alone. Second, name and grade the tenant behind every project, because spreads already follow the tenant. Third, stress the timing case in writing, with revenue arriving 12 to 24 months late and refinancing priced wider, and check who absorbs carry costs. Fourth, separate real end user demand from demand funded by a supplier or guarantor inside the same chain, and count only the first kind as proof. This requires no view on whether a bubble exists. It requires a view on where a loss would land.

Jengara decision rule

Fund AI capacity like credit, not like software. Approve a project only when the full obligation is visible in one place, the tenant can pay through a late revenue case, and the party that bears the loss on default is named before signing. If any of those three answers is unclear, the capacity is not yet an investment. It is an exposure.

Executive takeaway

The buildout is real, and so is the leverage beneath it. Advantage now belongs to buyers and investors who read guarantees and leases as carefully as they read model benchmarks.
