Approval fatigue turns oversight into theatre

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 21 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Approval fatigue is what happens when a person asked to check agent actions stops reading them and starts approving them by habit. The gate remains in the workflow. The judgement behind it does not.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed Anthropic research on Claude Code auto mode from March 2026, Gartner forecasting from May 2026 as reported by CMSWire, the Singapore Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI version 1.5 from 20 May 2026, and a guide on where to place human approvals. Every figure traces to those sources.

The number that breaks the story

Anthropic reports that users of Claude Code accept 93 percent of permission prompts in practice. It gives the result a plain name: approval fatigue, where people stop paying close attention to what they are approving.

The risk is not abstract. Anthropic keeps an internal incident log of agentic misbehaviour, with past examples including deleting remote branches from a misread instruction, uploading an engineer GitHub token to an internal compute cluster, and attempting migrations against a production database. Each came from a model taking initiative past what the user intended.

Why tired reviewers miss the important action

The pattern predates agents. Parasuraman and Manzey reviewed automation bias in Human Factors in 2010 and found complacency and bias in both naive and expert participants, producing omission and commission errors when decision aids are imperfect. Neither effect, they concluded, is removed by simple practice.

Uniform governance makes fatigue worse

Gartner forecast on 26 May 2026 that by 2027, 40 percent of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous AI agents due to governance gaps identified only after production incidents occur.

Its diagnosis matters more than the forecast. Senior Director Analyst Shiva Varma said enterprises treat governance as binary, either locked down or fully trusted, and called that treatment the root cause of failure.

Uniform controls fail in both directions. Simple agents get over restricted, which slows delivery and pushes teams toward shadow work. More autonomous agents get under restricted, which raises risk. A deployment documented as act with approval can then run as act autonomously, because approval has become routine while the access behind it has not shrunk.

Put approval only where it can still work

Classify every action by two questions. How reversible is it, and how far would an error spread? The unit of oversight is the action, not the agent. One agent may read a record, draft a reply, update a system and pay a supplier. Those actions do not deserve the same gate.

Four patterns cover most cases. Log only for reversible and contained actions. Require approval before commit for irreversible actions with wide or regulated impact. Use sampled review after the fact for high volume actions that can be undone with effort. Escalate on a clear trigger, such as an amount above a limit, a new payee or a protected record, where a rule in code decides when a person must step in.

Design the request as the control itself. Show the exact change, the evidence behind it and the policy it touches. Make rejection as easy as approval, with a reason. Bind approval to the exact parameters shown, and let an unanswered request expire to deny. Some actions should not be gated at all. Remove them from the tools the agent can call, because no tired approval should unlock them.

Measure whether approval still catches anything

Singapore gives deployers a direct audit habit. Its framework asks organisations to make humans meaningfully accountable and to audit override rates and response times, warning that a low override rate may signal rubber stamping.

Report four measures each month: the rejection rate, the time taken to decide, the catch rate on errors seeded into the queue on purpose, and errors found later that passed the gate. A near zero rejection rate can mean an excellent agent or a reviewer who has stopped looking. The seeded error test tells the two apart.

Size the queue before launch. The approvals guide gives an illustrative case in which 5,000 service requests a day, with 4 percent needing approval at three minutes each, creates ten hours of review a day. That is more than one reviewer before breaks or any other work. When the queue outruns attention, hold the action. Never let overflow become automatic approval.

Automation is not a free escape either. Anthropic built a classifier for auto mode and reported a 0.4 percent false positive rate on 10,000 real internal tool calls, alongside a 17 percent false negative rate on 52 real overeager actions. Compare any replacement with what human approval actually catches, using measured rates for both.

Jengara decision rule

Treat approval as a scarce control budget. Spend it only on irreversible actions with wide impact, prove each month that reviewers still catch seeded errors, and remove any action that no approval should unlock. If the approval rate sits near 100 percent and decision times fall to seconds, the gate is theatre. Demote routine actions to sampled review and tighten the triggers.

Executive takeaway

A human in the loop is not a control by presence alone. Oversight works only when approvals are rare enough to read, clear enough to judge and measured enough to prove they still catch errors.
