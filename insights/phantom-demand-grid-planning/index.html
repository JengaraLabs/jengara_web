Phantom demand is now a grid planning problem

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 24 October 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Phantom demand is power that has been requested on paper but may never be built. It is now a central problem in AI infrastructure, because grid plans, capital plans and household bills are being shaped by requests that utilities believe will not turn into real load at the scale claimed.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed Wood Mackenzie analysis reported by Bloomberg and The Wall Street Journal, the Boston Consulting Group report on flexible grid access published on 22 October 2026, and GlobalData reporting based on the International Energy Agency 2026 Electricity Outlook.

The queue is not the demand

Developers have requested 1,066 gigawatts of grid connections for data centers in the United States, according to Wood Mackenzie. That equals roughly 83 percent of the entire utility scale generating capacity of the country at the end of 2025.

Wood Mackenzie expects utilities to commit to only about 28 percent of that capacity. About 12 percent already holds firm service commitments, and a further 17 percent looks likely. Much of the rest is duplication and speculation.

How phantom load enters the queue

Developers who do not yet know which utility can deliver power fastest often file the same project with several utilities and keep every option open. Others hold queue positions without site control or a signed customer, betting that land with a grid connection attached will find a buyer. Commentary in POWER Magazine pointed to private equity funds, land brokers and shell companies among those claiming positions.

Scale makes sorting harder. Exelon told The Wall Street Journal that typical requests used to run around 50 megawatts and now often reach 1,000 megawatts or more. A few unreal projects can distort a regional plan.

Proof is becoming the price of a queue place

Utilities are now pricing the uncertainty, demanding higher rates, longer contracts, collateral and steep study fees before they reserve capacity. Exelon began requiring transmission security agreements that bind customers to credit obligations, committed revenue contributions and shortfall payments. Once that test applied, its high probability load fell from 18 gigawatts at the end of 2025 to about 11 gigawatts in the second quarter, a drop of nearly 40 percent. Its wider pipeline fell from about 43 gigawatts to 25 gigawatts. Projects that stayed have posted about 1 billion dollars in collateral.

Average minimum contract lengths have also risen to about 10.8 years, from 4.6 years before 2024, according to filings aggregated by Halcyon.

Why households and investors should care

Grid costs are shared. If a utility builds substations and transmission lines for a project that never arrives, other customers can end up paying for them. Phantom load is a ratepayer issue as well as a planning issue.

PJM, the largest grid operator in the United States, saw its capacity auction clear at the price cap for the third time in a row this summer while missing its reliability target by 6.8 gigawatts. In September its independent market monitor told federal regulators that 4,017 megawatts of forecast load, equal to 42 percent of the growth PJM had assumed, had already been cancelled or delayed. Plans built on unproven load can be wrong in both directions at once.

Flexible access offers a faster real path

The constructive answer is not only to filter harder. It is also to offer a different kind of connection. Boston Consulting Group reported on 22 October 2026 that data centers could cut waiting time from 5 to 10 years or more to 2 to 4 years by accepting flexible connections and using their own generation and storage. A flexible connection is non firm. Supply may be reduced in periods of congestion, and the operator covers those periods with on site generation, batteries and workloads that can shift in time.

The study covered five markets in Northwest Europe. GlobalData, drawing on the International Energy Agency 2026 Electricity Outlook, reports more than 2,500 gigawatts waiting in queues worldwide and waits of seven to 10 years in main European hubs.

For a modelled 150 megawatt AI inference site in Northwest Europe, connecting three years earlier could generate an estimated 1.28 billion dollars in additional profits, before the cost of on site power assets. An off grid setup could raise annual power costs by 70 to 230 percent compared with a firm connection. Flexibility pays workload by workload, not as a blanket substitute for firm power.

What leaders should do now

Four moves follow. First, separate committed megawatts from requested megawatts in every plan and investment case. Second, expect collateral, longer contracts and study fees, and budget for them as part of site cost. Third, split workloads by flexibility before choosing a connection, placing training and batch work that can pause or shift ahead of latency sensitive inference that cannot. Fourth, ask one question in writing: what evidence makes this load high confidence, and what happens to grid costs if it does not arrive.

Jengara decision rule

Plan on committed megawatts, not requested megawatts. If a project cannot post money, name a customer and accept a long firm contract or a flexible connection, treat its power as an option, never as capacity.

Executive takeaway

The queue used to measure ambition. Now it measures uncertainty, until money turns a request into load.
