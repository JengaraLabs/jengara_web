The memory wall: AI scale now runs on memory

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 20 October 2026 · Research and executive analysis

The memory wall is the point where AI scale stops being set by how many accelerators you can buy and starts being set by how much memory you can secure. That point has arrived. The stacked memory beside the accelerator is allocated years ahead, priced like a scarce asset, and quietly repricing everything from servers to laptops.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the September 2026 analysis from the Center for Strategic and International Studies, forecasts from IDC and Gartner, Reuters reporting on SK hynix, and public statements from Micron leadership. Every figure in this note traces to one of those sources, and the sources are listed in full below.

The scarce input moved one layer down

For two years the industry planned around accelerator supply. That planning now misses the binding constraint. The Center for Strategic and International Studies projects that data centers will consume approximately 70 percent of all memory devices produced worldwide in 2026. The mechanism is substitution inside the fab. High bandwidth memory, the stacked memory that feeds AI accelerators, is made in the same facilities and from the same silicon wafers as conventional DRAM. Producers therefore reallocate existing cleanroom capacity rather than add new capacity, and each reallocation removes commodity memory from the market. The exchange rate is steep. Producing high bandwidth memory requires roughly four times the cleanroom capacity per gigabyte of standard DRAM, according to the same CSIS analysis. Demand is also concentrated. A small group of large cloud providers, including Meta, Google, Microsoft and Amazon, negotiate long term supply agreements at premium prices, which gives manufacturers stable and lucrative demand while leaving other buyers exposed to price volatility and uncertain supply.

Supply growth cannot close the gap quickly

IDC expects DRAM supply to grow about 16 percent in 2026 and NAND supply about 17 percent, both below historical norms, while demand grows far faster. New fabrication plants take years to build and ramp, so relief arrives on a calendar that AI buyers do not control. The SK hynix chief executive expects the current memory shortage to persist through the end of 2030, Reuters reported in August 2026, and the company plans to begin volume production of its next generation memory at its Indiana facility only in the third quarter of 2029. That project costs more than four billion dollars and still lands late in the decade. Micron tells the same story from the seller side. Its leadership has said the company can meet only about half to two thirds of demand from key customers, that supply will be significantly tighter in 2027 and 2028 than in 2026, and that it has no line of sight to when supply and demand return to balance. When the sellers cannot see the end, buyers should not plan as if they can.

The bill lands far beyond the data center

Gartner estimates that combined DRAM and solid state drive prices will rise 130 percent by the end of 2026. The firm expects average PC prices to rise 17 percent and smartphone prices 13 percent against 2025, while worldwide PC shipments fall 10.4 percent and smartphone shipments fall 8.4 percent. Memory is also taking a larger share of each device. Gartner puts memory at 23 percent of the total PC bill of materials in 2026, up from 16 percent in 2025, and expects the entry level PC segment priced below 500 dollars to disappear by 2028 because vendors can no longer absorb the cost. For enterprise buyers the lesson is direct. A memory shortage that begins beside an accelerator ends inside laptop refresh budgets, device lifetimes and support risk, because older fleets stay in service longer.

Plan memory the way you plan power

Four moves follow. First, treat memory as an allocated input in every capacity plan. Ask suppliers what share of your requirement is actually committed, in writing, and for which quarters, rather than accepting a unit price and a delivery estimate. Second, stress test budgets against the Gartner price path instead of last year pricing, and include device fleets alongside server orders, because both draw on the same constrained supply. Third, reduce the memory each workload needs before buying more of it. Test smaller models, shorter context, caching and lower precision formats against your own quality bar, and measure the memory saved per successful task. Efficiency work that once looked optional now buys real capacity. Fourth, watch advanced packaging as closely as wafer supply. CSIS notes that packaging capacity is essential to producing the stacked memory AI systems use, so a wafer without packaging is not deliverable memory. Boards should ask where packaging sits in each supplier plan.

Jengara decision rule

Allocate for scarcity. If a growth plan assumes abundant and cheap memory in 2027, rewrite the plan. Secure committed supply early, design workloads to use less memory, and budget at prices that reflect rationing rather than abundance.

Executive takeaway

The accelerator roadmap is no longer the plan. The memory allocation behind it is.
