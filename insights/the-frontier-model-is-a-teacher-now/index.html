The frontier model is a teacher now

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 25 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Distillation is the practice of training a small model on the outputs of a larger one, so the small model learns how the larger one behaves on a specific job. The result reframes the frontier model. Its highest value use is no longer answering every request. It is teaching a cheaper model to answer most of them.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the Stanford AI Index 2025, a Gartner forecast from April 2025, TensorZero research from July 2025, NVIDIA material on its Data Flywheel Blueprint, and a National CIO Review synthesis. Vendor results are attributed and treated as results on the tasks tested, not as general promises.

The price of a fixed level of ability collapsed

Stanford reports that the cost of querying a model at the ability level of GPT 3.5 fell from 20 dollars per million tokens in November 2022 to 0.07 dollars by October 2024, a fall of more than 280 times. The comparison holds ability constant. It tracks the price of reaching a set level of performance, not one model getting cheaper.

The model that reached the lowest price in that comparison was a small model, Gemini 1.5 Flash 8B. Much of the celebrated fall in inference cost is therefore not the frontier getting cheap. It is smaller models delivering an older frontier level of ability at a fraction of the original price. Yesterday ability is becoming a commodity. Current frontier ability remains the expensive input.

What the teacher actually transfers

In 2015 Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals and Jeff Dean formalised the teacher and student method, in which a large model transfers its knowledge, including the subtle probability judgments it makes between answers, into a much smaller model that is cheaper to deploy. The student learns more than final answers. It learns how the teacher ranks plausible answers.

The early proof still reads well. DistilBERT retained 97 percent of the language understanding performance of BERT with 40 percent fewer parameters, while running 60 percent faster. For a bounded job, much of what a large model knows can be compressed into a model sized for that job, provided the training examples show the job clearly.

Evidence from production shaped tasks

TensorZero tested fine tuning small models on programmatically curated data drawn from larger model outputs. Across data extraction, multi turn navigation, agentic retrieval and agentic tool use, the fine tuned models achieved cost reductions of up to 30 times while improving or maintaining competitive performance. Its central finding was about curation. Selecting and shaping the training examples mattered more than simply collecting more of them.

NVIDIA reports a sharper result on a narrower job. In its Data Flywheel Blueprint, a fine tuned Llama 3.2 model with one billion parameters achieved 98 percent of the tool calling accuracy of a Llama 3.3 model with 70 billion parameters. The smaller model needed one GPU to serve, against two for the larger model, and NVIDIA reports an inference cost reduction above 98 percent on that task. Treat both results as task specific. That is precisely the point.

Why enterprises will run students at volume

Gartner predicts that by 2027 organisations will use small task specific models at a usage volume at least three times that of general purpose large language models, because the smaller models respond more quickly and use less computing power. The forecast fits enterprise work. Most volume is repeated work: classification, extraction, summarisation, routing, drafting in a fixed format, and calling a known set of tools.

Repeated work makes the economics close. Training a student is a fixed cost. Serving it is a marginal cost paid on every request. At low volume the fixed cost never earns back. At high volume on a stable job, it earns back quickly and then keeps paying. Distillation is therefore a portfolio decision. Which workloads are frequent enough, stable enough and measurable enough to justify a student of their own?

Where distillation fails or misleads

Distillation narrows scope. A student trained to excel at one job should not be trusted outside it, and the sources reviewed agree that most systems still need a frontier model paired with the student for edge cases and open reasoning. Removing the teacher removes both the fallback and the source of future training data.

Students also inherit teacher errors. If flawed teacher outputs enter training unfiltered, the student learns the error as doctrine and delivers it more cheaply and more often. Curation and evaluation decide whether distillation transfers capability or transfers a flaw. Governance matters as well. Legitimate distillation in the sources reviewed relies on your own production traffic or a managed tool offered by the provider. Building a student from a competitor service in breach of its terms is extraction, not engineering.

How to adopt it in the right order

Start with one repeated job that has volume and a clear test of success. Log frontier model outputs on your own traffic for that job, with the outcomes that followed. Curate hard, and hold back a test set drawn from real work that training never sees.

Measure the student on cost per success, not cost per token. A cheap model that fails more often can cost more per completed job once retries, fallbacks and human correction are counted. Set a quality threshold the student must clear before it serves live traffic, and keep the frontier model in the path as fallback. Then keep teaching. Jobs drift and definitions move, so schedule fresh distillation from current production traffic and compare each new student against the one in production before promotion. The flywheel, not the first student, is the durable asset.

Jengara decision rule

Use the frontier model as teacher and fallback, and a distilled student as the runtime, when a workload is high volume, stable in shape and measurable by a clear success test. Prove the student on real work held back from training, and judge it on cost per success. If the job is rare, shifting or open in shape, rent the frontier and skip the student.

Executive takeaway

The frontier model is not becoming irrelevant. Its job is changing. Enterprises that treat it only as a runtime will keep paying frontier prices for routine work. Enterprises that treat it as a teacher will own cheaper capability that improves every time production traffic teaches it again.
