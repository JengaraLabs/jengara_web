An agent on a shared account is unaccountable

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 5 November 2026 · Product and engineering notes

An agent identity is the named and owned credential that lets one specific agent act in specific systems for a specific purpose, and lets investigators later prove exactly what that agent did. An agent that borrows a human login or shares a service account has no such identity, and its actions merge with those of every other user of that account.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed the CyberArk 2025 Identity Security Landscape announcement, Gartner forecasts from June and August 2025, the Anthropic disclosure of November 2025, Okta guidance on identity first agent security, and the OWASP GenAI Security Project Top 10. Every figure traces to those sources.

The count has already changed

CyberArk reported in its 2025 Identity Security Landscape that machine identities now outnumber human identities by 82 to 1, in a study drawing on 2,600 cybersecurity decision makers. In the same study, 88 percent of respondents said the definition of a privileged user applied solely to human identities, while 42 percent of machine identities held privileged or sensitive access.

Gartner expects the agent share to grow quickly. In August 2025 it forecast that 40 percent of enterprise applications will feature task specific AI agents by the end of 2026, up from less than 5 percent in 2025.

A shared account erases the witness

Access control answers three questions. Who acted. Under whose authority. Within what limits. A shared account answers none of them. The audit log records the account name, and that name carries no accountability. When an agent on a shared account deletes records or reads data it should never have seen, investigators cannot tell which agent acted or which owner approved its access.

Okta treats each agent as a non human identity that needs defined ownership, scoped permissions and lifecycle governance. It cites the Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report for 2025, in which compromised credentials were the initial access vector in 22 percent of confirmed breaches.

Autonomy raises the value of identity

Anthropic disclosed in November 2025 that a group it designated GTG 1002, which it assessed with high confidence was a Chinese state sponsored group, manipulated Claude Code into attempting infiltration of roughly thirty global targets and succeeded in a small number of cases. It estimated that AI performed 80 to 90 percent of the campaign, with human intervention at only a few critical decision points.

The lesson for defenders is about tempo. An agent can complete in minutes a chain of actions a person would take days to perform. Controls that depend on a person noticing each action arrive after the action has finished. Identity works at that speed, because scope, expiry and revocation attach to the credential before the first action runs. You cannot revoke what you cannot name.

Excessive agency is an identity failure in practice

The OWASP GenAI Security Project ranks prompt injection first in its Top 10 for LLM Applications and lists excessive agency among the leading risks. Excessive agency describes a system given more functionality, permissions or autonomy than the task requires. In the 2026 edition, excessive agency climbed from sixth place to third, a move Imperva attributes to agentic applications that take actions moving into production.

Read together, the two risks describe one event in two parts. Prompt injection supplies the hostile instruction. Excessive agency supplies the power to carry it out. Damage from a manipulated agent is set by the permissions its identity carries at that moment. Scoping an identity tightly is not administrative hygiene.

Give every agent a lifecycle, not a login

Start with a register. Every production agent gets one unique identity, one named human owner, a written purpose, the systems it may reach and the classes of data it may touch. If an agent cannot be found in the register, it does not run in production.

Issue credentials for the task and for a limited time, scoped separately for each environment, and never place a credential inside a prompt, a document or source code. Separate reading from writing and writing from approving, so one identity cannot request an action and also authorise it. Require human approval before irreversible actions such as payment, deletion or external sending. Log every tool call under the agent identity and revoke it when the purpose ends.

What to test before you scale

Gartner forecast in June 2025 that over 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls. Identity failure sits inside the third cause, because an incident involving an unattributable agent forces the whole programme to slow while trust is rebuilt.

Three tests expose the position. Can the team name every agent now running in production, with its owner and its largest permission. Can the team revoke one agent within minutes without breaking others who share its account. Can investigators replay one run from logs that tie each action to one identity. A no to any test means the deployment is still a pilot.

Jengara decision rule

Give no agent a shared account, a standing broad permission or a credential without an owner and an expiry date. Scale only agents whose identity lets you answer who acted, under whose authority, within what limits and how that access ends. If an action cannot be attributed to one agent and one owner, the agent is not ready for production.

Executive takeaway

Agent adoption is adding actors to the enterprise faster than identity programmes were designed to absorb. Organisations that scale safely will issue, scope and retire agent identities with the discipline they apply to people.
