Waterless cooling is now a siting test for AI data centers

By Frank Ribeiro, Jengara · 22 October 2026 · Product and engineering notes

Waterless cooling is the shift from evaporating water to cool AI chips to recirculating a sealed fluid and rejecting heat to outside air. It sounds like a facilities detail. It is now a siting decision, because the amount of water a campus takes from a town can decide whether that campus gets built at all, and on what terms.

Research approach

Jengara reviewed statements and reporting on the NVIDIA DSX design, the Microsoft closed loop programme and Meta liquid cooling work in Texas, alongside the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory 2024 data center energy report and International Energy Agency analysis. Every figure traces to a source listed below.

The water bill was hiding inside the cooling bill

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that data centers in the United States directly consumed about 17.4 billion gallons of water in 2023, mostly for cooling, and projected that direct use could reach 38 to 73 billion gallons a year by 2028 as AI campuses multiply. NVIDIA puts conventional cooling tower consumption at about 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year, which is roughly 260 million gallons a year for a 100 megawatt campus. NVIDIA also notes that cooling has historically accounted for up to 40 percent of a data center electricity use. Water and power were never separate bills.

Warmer coolant is the engineering unlock

The breakthrough in the new designs is temperature, not plumbing alone. In the NVIDIA DSX reference design for its Rubin generation systems, coolant enters the servers at 45 degrees Celsius and leaves at about 55 degrees after absorbing chip heat. The coolant is a mix of 75 percent water and 25 percent propylene glycol, filled once and recirculated through cold plates fitted directly on the processors. Because the coolant arrives warm, outdoor dry coolers can reject its heat in many climates without mechanical chillers or evaporative towers. NVIDIA director of data center cooling Ali Heydari said the design has zero water consumption, while noting that chillers may still be needed for about 1 percent of the year in some climates. Industry estimates cited by NVIDIA put the saving from each degree rise in chiller plant temperature at about 4 percent of cooling energy cost, and the company estimates that a 50 megawatt site could save more than 4 million dollars a year in cooling related energy and water costs.

Operators are making it a standard, not an option

Microsoft reached the same design conclusion earlier and turned it into policy. Starting in August 2024, all new Microsoft data center designs began using chip level cooling that recirculates water in a closed loop with zero water evaporation for cooling. Microsoft said the design avoids the need for more than 125 million litres of water per year per data center, with pilot sites in Phoenix, Arizona and Mt. Pleasant, Wisconsin, and new sites using the design due to come online in late 2027. Meta is moving the same way. In August 2026 Meta detailed closed loop liquid cooling at its Texas data center, said the coolant can be used for up to a decade before replacement, and said an AI optimized data center using dry coolers consumes less water annually than two full service restaurants. When operators of this scale converge on one design, cooling becomes the expected baseline for a credible planning application.

The catch is the water inside the electricity

A sealed loop solves only the water that a campus draws itself. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory put the water consumed generating the electricity used by United States data centers at about 211 billion gallons in 2023, roughly twelve times the direct on site figure. That hidden share grows as power demand grows. The International Energy Agency put global data center electricity consumption at 485 terawatt hours in 2025 and projected about 950 terawatt hours by 2030. So a campus can report near zero cooling water while its total footprint still rises, if the local grid makes its power with water intensive generation. On site water tells a town what leaves its taps. Embedded water tells investors what the campus demands from the wider system.

Treat cooling as a permit strategy

Four moves follow. First, ask for two numbers in writing, by year: on site water consumption and the water embedded in the electricity supply. A single combined figure hides the trade this technology creates. Second, design for warm coolant from the start. That choice is far cheaper at design stage than as a retrofit once towers and chillers are already bought. Third, score climate honestly. NVIDIA itself allows for chiller use on a small share of days in hotter climates, so model the hottest week rather than the average week. Fourth, publish measured use once the site is running, not only design intent. Operators who can show metered near zero cooling water hold a real advantage in contested planning processes.

Jengara decision rule

Approve the site on total water, not on cooling water alone. If a proposal cannot show both the sealed loop at the rack and the water cost of the power behind it, in measured numbers a community can check, treat the water claim as unfinished.

Executive takeaway

Cooling used to be chosen by engineers after a site was picked. Now it helps pick the site.
